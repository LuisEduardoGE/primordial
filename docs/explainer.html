Primordial · plain-language guide

A digital petri dish where behaviour evolves by itself

Primordial simulates a few hundred to a few thousand tiny creatures. Each one has a small artificial brain. Nobody tells them what to do. Over time, the ones that happen to behave well leave more offspring, and their behaviour spreads. This page explains what you are looking at, step by step.

The short version

  1. Every dot on the screen is a particle: a simple creature with a body, an energy reserve and a brain.
  2. Living costs energy. A particle gets energy back by eating food (the small dots) or by attacking other particles.
  3. A particle with enough energy splits in two. The child gets a copy of the parent's brain and body, with a few small random changes.
  4. A particle that runs out of energy dies.
  5. Repeat this millions of times. Changes that help a particle survive get passed on; changes that hurt it disappear. That is evolution, and it is the only thing shaping their behaviour.

Meet one particle

A particle is a circle that moves around a flat world. When it reaches one edge it comes back on the opposite side, like in old arcade games, so there are no walls. Each particle has three parts.

Body
How big it is, how fast it can move and how far it can see. Bigger bodies store more energy and can overpower smaller ones, but they burn more energy just staying alive. Better eyes and faster legs also have running costs. Every advantage has a price.
Brain
A tiny neural network: about 25 inputs, 16 internal units and 9 outputs. It is the same basic idea used in modern AI, just very small. It starts out random, so newborn creatures of the first generation behave aimlessly.
Energy
The particle's fuel and its score at the same time. Running out means death. Collecting enough means reproduction.

One moment in its life

Every step of the simulation, each particle does the same four things:

1. Sense
Where is the nearest food? Are there relatives nearby, or strangers? Is a stranger bigger than me? Is someone signalling? Is there a scent trail, and which way does it get stronger? How much energy do I have left? What season is it?
2. Decide
The brain combines all those readings and produces its decisions. It also keeps two numbers of short-term memory, so it can react to what happened a moment ago.
3. Act
It pushes itself in some direction, may attack a stranger it is touching, may glow to signal others, may drop scent, and may bond with a relative it is touching.
4. Pay
Energy is subtracted for being alive, for its body, and for every action. If it is touching food, it eats and gains energy.

How does it get better without being taught?

Think of a recipe that gets photocopied over and over, where each copy picks up a small random typo. Most typos make the dish slightly worse. Once in a while one makes it better. Now imagine that cooks who make better dishes get to hand out more copies. After many rounds, the recipes in circulation are good ones, even though nobody ever sat down to improve them.

That is exactly what happens here. The "recipe" is a particle's genome: its brain's settings and its body measurements. When a particle splits, its child's genome is copied with small random changes called mutations. Children whose changes help them find food or avoid predators live longer and split more often, so those changes spread. Two compatible relatives can also mate, and their child mixes both parents' genomes.

How big should those random changes be? We tested a version where each creature also inherits its own mutation size, so evolution could tune how adventurous its copying errors are. It sounded clever, but it did not help (see the results below), so the simulation now uses a fixed mutation size. The clever version is still available as an option for experiments.

Where is the machine learning?

Most AI you hear about learns from a large collection of examples. Primordial has no examples and no teacher. It uses two kinds of learning instead.

Learning across generations
Evolution, as described above. The technical name is neuroevolution: improving neural networks by selection and mutation instead of by training on data.
Learning within a lifetime
Each brain can also adjust itself while the particle is alive. When an action is followed by a gain in energy, the connections that produced it get slightly stronger; when energy drops, they weaken. This is a simple form of reward-based learning. How quickly each particle learns is itself an inherited trait.
Recognising species
A separate algorithm (k-means clustering) looks at what every particle is and does, groups similar ones together, and gives each group a name. Nobody defines the species in advance.

What tends to emerge

None of these strategies is written into the program. They appear because they work. The automatic species detector names groups by what they do:

Grazers

Get almost all their energy from food. Usually the majority.

Hunters

Get a large share of their energy by attacking other particles. They need to be big enough to overpower their prey.

Colonial

Particles that stay bonded to relatives and share energy along the bonds, forming chains and clusters that behave like small multi-celled organisms.

Signallers

Particles that glow strongly. Others can sense the glow, so signals can come to mean things like "food here".

Trail-layers

Particles that leave scent behind them, which others can follow, much like ants.

In one run of the desktop version, after about 2,000 steps, the detector counted 122 grazers, 56 particles in a second grazer group, 51 signallers, 34 colonial particles and 6 hunters.

Reading the screen

Circle size shows body size.

Colour shows family. Relatives have similar colours, and colours drift slowly from generation to generation.

Red outline: this particle is attacking right now.

Line between particles: a bond. Bonded particles move together and share energy.

Small dots are food. Most food grows in a few fertile areas that drift slowly around the world.

Faint ring: a fertile area. Food is richer there and it slowly moves, so creatures must keep finding it.

Food also grows faster and slower in a slow cycle of seasons, so a strategy that works in plenty might fail in a lean season.

What has been measured so far

To check that evolution is really producing better behaviour, evolved particles were placed in a fresh world together with particles that had random brains, 100 of each, and left to compete.

ExperimentResultWhat it means
Evolved brains vs random brains5 of 5 trialsThe random group died out every time. Evolution clearly produces better survivors.
"Smart" mutation vs simple mutation (8 separate worlds each)simple won in 6 of 8 worldsThe smarter-sounding method, where creatures also evolve how much they mutate, did not do better. If anything the simple method did slightly better, but the difference is not large enough to be sure.
Searching for interesting worlds (24 worlds tried)9 distinct kinds foundThe richest ecosystems had plenty of food, strong seasons, and creatures that share energy with their relatives.

Why run the same test in 8 worlds? Evolution involves chance. One world can favour a method by luck, so a fair comparison repeats the test in many independently started worlds and checks whether the difference holds up. In the earlier two-world test, one world favoured each method, which is exactly the kind of luck this guards against.

How to run it

There are two versions. The desktop version (Python) is the research tool: it can run experiments, record videos and draw the family tree. The browser version runs a larger world, around 3,000 to 4,500 particles, with nothing to install.

cd primordial
.venv/Scripts/python -m primordial view                     # watch it live
.venv/Scripts/python -m primordial run --steps 20000 --save run.pt
.venv/Scripts/python -m primordial timelapse --load run.pt --out evolution.mp4

In the desktop viewer, click any particle to see its body, its family and its brain working live. Press F for the family tree and G for the settings panel.

Glossary

Genome
Everything a particle inherits: brain settings, body measurements, colour, how readily it learns, and how likely its children are to stay attached to it.
Mutation
A small random change made when a genome is copied to a child.
Generation
How many parents separate a particle from the first, randomly created ancestor.
Neural network
A web of simple connected units that turns numbers in (senses) into numbers out (actions). The connection strengths decide its behaviour.
Lineage
A family line. "Effective lineages" estimates how many distinct families share the world right now.
Predation share
The fraction of all energy that came from attacking other particles rather than from food.
Pheromone
Scent left on the ground. It spreads out and fades over time.
Abiogenesis
If the population ever falls too low, new random particles are added so the world never empties. A healthy world rarely needs this.
